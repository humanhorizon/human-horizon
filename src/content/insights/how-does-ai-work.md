How does AI work? It finds patterns in a very large number of examples, then uses those patterns to produce an answer for something new. A chatbot, for instance, has learned from huge amounts of text how words tend to follow each other, and it uses that to write a reply to your question. In our reading, it is producing a likely reply rather than checking facts, which is why you should check what it tells you.

## What counts as AI?

There is no single rulebook, but one well-known definition comes from [the OECD](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/definition), which describes an AI system as a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from the input it receives how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments. In everyday terms: you give it something, and it works out a useful output, rather than following a fixed list of steps someone wrote in advance.

## How does an AI learn?

One way AI systems are built is machine learning, which [the OECD describes](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/definition) as techniques that let machines improve their performance through exposure to training data. Show a system thousands of emails marked "spam" and "not spam", and it picks up what the spam ones tend to have in common. Nobody writes the rule "an email with these words is spam". The system works out the pattern itself, and then applies it to emails it has never seen.

## How does a chatbot write an answer?

Many chatbots are built on language models, which are trained on a very large amount of text. [The OECD gives an example](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/definition) (page published March 2024): a language model is given large amounts of language and the objective to "predict the next word". When you type a question it builds its reply the same way, choosing what is likely to come next, which is why the answer usually sounds fluent and relevant.

## Why can it be wrong?

In our reading, because it is built to predict what fits, it is not checking what is true. An answer can be out of date, wrong or not right for your situation, and still read as confident. It can also miss context that you know and it does not, such as your customer, your policy or your local rules. So a person should check anything that matters.

## What is AI good at, and what is it not?

In our view it is good at work with lots of language or patterns: drafting, summarising, sorting, pulling details out of documents, suggesting options. It is weaker where facts must be exactly right, where the situation is new, or where a judgement involves people. A fair rule for work: let it do the first draft, and you do the checking.

## What should you do with this?

Use AI for the routine part of a task, and keep the decision for yourself. Give it the context it needs, read what it produces as you would a colleague's first draft, and do not paste in anything your company treats as confidential unless your policy says you can. Your employer may have its own rules, so ask.

## Questions people ask

**Does AI think?** Not in the human sense. It finds patterns and produces likely outputs.

**Does AI look things up on the internet?** Some tools can search the web. A language model on its own answers from what it learned in training. Check whether your tool says where an answer came from.
