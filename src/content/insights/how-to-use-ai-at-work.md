How to use AI at work: start with one small task you already do every week, such as a summary, a first draft or a list. Give the AI tool the background, say what you want back, read the result critically and keep client or staff information out unless your company has said it is allowed. That is the whole method. The rest of this guide shows where to apply it first.

## Why start with a task, not a tool

A common pattern is to open an AI assistant, type something vague and get something vague back, then decide AI is not for you. The tool is rarely the problem. The problem is that the task was not picked or described.

Pick something you do often, that takes you real time, and where a first draft helps more than a blank page. You stay in charge of the final version.

## Seven everyday tasks to try first

- **Summarise a long document or email thread.** Paste in the text (if it is safe to share, see below) and ask for the five points a busy reader needs, plus any decisions or dates.
- **Draft a first version of an email.** Say who it is for, what you need from them and the tone. Edit it until it sounds like you.
- **Turn rough notes into clean minutes.** Paste your meeting notes and ask for decisions, owners and next steps in a table.
- **Prepare for a meeting.** Ask for the questions someone in that role is likely to raise about your proposal, then prepare answers.
- **Rewrite for a different reader.** Ask for the same message for a customer, a manager and a new joiner, and compare.
- **Make a checklist or outline.** Describe the job and ask for the steps in order. Use it as a starting point and correct it from experience.
- **Look at a spreadsheet.** Describe your columns and ask what to check or how to build a summary formula. Check the answer on a few rows yourself.

## How to ask: four parts

A clear request has four parts. You do not need special wording.

- **The job:** what you want done, such as "summarise this" or "draft a reply".
- **The background:** who the reader is and what they already know.
- **The format:** a short paragraph, five bullets, a table.
- **The limits:** length, tone, what to leave out.

For example: "Summarise the notes below for my manager, who missed the meeting. Five bullets, then a list of decisions and who owns each. Keep it under 150 words."

If the first answer is not right, say what to change. Asking again with one fix usually works better than starting over.

## Check the answer before you use it

AI tools can sound sure and still be wrong. They can invent a figure, a quote or a source. Treat the output as a draft from a fast colleague who has not seen your files.

- Check every number, name and date against the original.
- Open any source it gives you, and drop it if you cannot find it.
- Read it aloud. If it does not sound like you or your company, change it.

The more the answer matters, the more time the check deserves.

## What should I not paste into an AI tool?

Before you put anything into an AI tool, ask three questions.

- Has my company said which tools I may use for work, and on which account?
- Does this text name a customer, a colleague or anyone who could be identified?
- Would I be comfortable if this text were read outside the company?

If you are not sure, ask your manager, or remove the names and details first. Singapore's data protection regulator, the PDPC, published [Advisory Guidelines on Use of Personal Data in AI Recommendation and Decision Systems](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/-/media/files/pdpc/pdf-files/advisory-guidelines/advisory-guidelines-on-the-use-of-personal-data-in-ai-recommendation-and-decision-systems.pdf) in March 2024. They are written for organisations that build or deploy AI systems, not for individual staff, so pass them to whoever sets your company's rules.

## A worked example

Imagine you are an operations coordinator who writes a weekly update from five team chat threads. Today it takes about an hour. A first try with AI:

1. Remove customer names and any private details from the threads.
2. Paste them in with the request: "Write a weekly update for the leadership team. Three headings: done, in progress, blocked. Short bullets. Plain language."
3. Read the draft against the threads. Fix anything that is wrong or missing.
4. Add the names back yourself, then send.

Whether this saves you time depends on your threads and your checking. Time the first two or three rounds and see.

Pick one task from the list and try it three times this week, and note what you change each time, because that shows what to put in the request next time.
