How to learn AI for work: you do not need to code or study for a degree. You need regular practice on the tasks you already do, a few habits for checking the answers, and an honest look at what changed after a month. This plan gives you four weeks to build that habit, using about 20 to 30 minutes on each working day.

## What learning AI means here

There are two different goals behind the same search. One is building AI systems, which is a technical path and needs maths and programming. The other is using AI tools well in your job, which needs judgement, clear instructions and care with information. This guide is about the second.

If you want the first, a university programme or a technical course is the right place to look, and this plan will not replace it.

## Before week one: choose your tasks

Write down five tasks you repeat at work. Mark the ones that involve reading, writing, sorting or summarising, because those are where AI tools tend to help first. Choose two to start with. If you want ideas, our seven everyday AI tasks to start with is a good list. Everything in the plan uses those two.

Also ask your manager which AI tools your company allows and what information must stay out. This takes five minutes and saves you from the one mistake that matters most.

## Week one: get comfortable

Use an approved AI assistant on your first task every day. Practise the basic request: the job, the background, the format and the limits. After each try, write one line on what you changed in the answer.

By Friday you should know what a decent answer looks like for that task, and what a poor one looks like.

## Week two: learn to check

This week is about trust. For each answer, check numbers, names and dates against the original and look for anything the tool added that you did not give it. AI tools can produce confident answers that are false, so the checking habit is the first one to build.

Keep a short list of the mistakes you catch. It will show you where the tool is weak for your kind of work.

## Week three: add the second task and improve your requests

Move to your second task. Now compare. Which request wording gave the best first draft? Save the best two as templates in a note, with a gap where the details go.

Try asking for different versions, such as shorter, plainer or for a different reader, and pick the closest. You are learning to steer the tool, not to accept its first answer.

## Week four: measure and decide

Time yourself on both tasks with and without AI, using real work. Include the time you spend checking. Write down three numbers: the time before, the time now, and how many fixes the drafts needed.

Then decide what to keep. Some tasks will improve, some will not, and both results are useful. Share your two templates and your results with a colleague, because explaining it to someone else shows you the gaps in what you know.

## How to tell it is working

- You reach for the tool without deciding to, on the tasks where it helps.
- You catch errors before anyone else does.
- Your requests are clearer and need fewer rounds.
- You can say which tasks AI is not good for.

None of this needs a certificate. If you later want structured training, our guide to [why AI courses fade](/insights/why-ai-courses-fade) explains why training often does not stick.

## Where this plan falls short

In our view, self-practice suits individual habits. It does less for team workflows, for safe data handling across a company, or for roles where the tools need setting up. In those cases, structured training on your own tasks is worth considering. If you are weighing a course, see what an AI course for beginners should cover.
