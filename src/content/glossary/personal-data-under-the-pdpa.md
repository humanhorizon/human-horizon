## How it works

PDPC's [Advisory Guidelines on Key Concepts in the PDPA](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/-/media/files/pdpc/pdf-files/advisory-guidelines/ag-on-key-concepts/advisory-guidelines-on-key-concepts-in-the-pdpa-17-may-2022.pdf) explain the Act's definition: data, whether true or not, about an individual who can be identified from it, or from it together with other information the organisation has or is likely to have access to. Some data identifies a person on its own, such as an NRIC number or a face in a photo. Other data, such as age or gender, can become personal data when combined with something that points to a particular person.

PDPC's [guide to the PDPA](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/-/media/Files/PDPC/PDF-Files/Resource-for-Individuals/what-you-need-to-know-about-pdpa-v1-0.pdf) gives everyday examples: full name, NRIC or passport number, a photo or video of a person, mobile number, personal email address, thumbprint, and name with residential address. The same guide says the PDPA covers electronic and non-electronic personal data, whether it is true or false.

Two points often surprise people:

- **Business contact information is treated differently.** Contact details given for business purposes, such as name, job title and business phone, address or email, are not covered by the data protection obligations, unless the person gave them solely for personal purposes.
- **Anonymised data is outside the PDPA, but not risk-free.** PDPC's [Advisory Guidelines on the Use of Personal Data in AI Recommendation and Decision Systems](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/-/media/files/pdpc/pdf-files/advisory-guidelines/advisory-guidelines-on-the-use-of-personal-data-in-ai-recommendation-and-decision-systems.pdf) say anonymised data is not subject to the PDPA, but still carries a risk of people being re-identified.

## What it means when you use AI

Those guidelines, issued on 1 March 2024, focus on systems that use machine learning to make recommendations, predictions or decisions. They are advisory, not legally binding. Points that matter for most teams:

- The PDPA applies when personal data is used to develop, test or run AI systems.
- Organisations generally need meaningful consent, unless an exception applies, such as the business improvement exception the guidelines describe.
- Organisations are encouraged to use only the personal data they need, and to pseudonymise or de-identify it where possible.
- Vendors who build custom AI systems using an organisation's personal data have their own PDPA obligations, and are encouraged to help the organisation meet its obligations.

The organisation does not hand its duties to the vendor. PDPC's [Advisory Guidelines on Key Concepts in the PDPA](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/-/media/files/pdpc/pdf-files/advisory-guidelines/ag-on-key-concepts/advisory-guidelines-on-key-concepts-in-the-pdpa-17-may-2022.pdf) quote the Act: an organisation is responsible for personal data in its possession or under its control.

For everyday AI assistants, the practical rule is simple. Know what personal data is, and keep it out of tools your company has not approved.

For the rules behind this, see [AI acceptable use policy](/insights/glossary/ai-acceptable-use-policy) and [shadow AI](/insights/glossary/shadow-ai).

## An example

An HR manager at a retail company wants AI to find themes in exit interview notes. Before using the approved tool, she removes names, employee numbers and other details that could identify anyone. The AI groups the comments into themes. She reads the summary against a sample of the original notes before sharing it with management.
