## How it works

Older AI tools mostly sorted or predicted things. They flagged an email as spam or forecast next month's sales. Generative AI makes something new. You type a request in plain language, and it writes a draft, summarises a document, suggests a reply or produces an image.

It does this by learning patterns from a very large body of text, images or other material. When you ask it something, it predicts what a useful response should look like, piece by piece. That is why it sounds fluent and confident. It is also why it can be wrong: it produces what looks likely, not what it has checked to be true.

The best-known tools are chat assistants, where you type and it replies. Generative AI also sits inside software many teams already use, such as email, documents and spreadsheets.

In Singapore, IMDA and the AI Verify Foundation published a [Model AI Governance Framework for Generative AI](https://aiverifyfoundation.sg/resources/mgf-gen-ai/) in May 2024. It sets out nine areas, from accountability and security to using AI for the public good, that organisations can use to think about trusted use.

## An example

A ten-person interior design firm in Singapore sends a written proposal after every site visit. A designer used to spend an evening on each one. Now generative AI turns the designer's site notes and the firm's past proposals into a full first draft, with scope, materials and next steps. The designer reads it, corrects the details and the tone, then sends it. The writing that used to take an evening becomes a careful review.

## Generative AI vs traditional AI

Traditional AI usually gives you a label, a score or a forecast from structured data. Generative AI gives you a new piece of content. Both are useful. Generative AI is the one most office teams will touch directly, because it works through plain language.
