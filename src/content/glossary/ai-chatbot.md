## How it works

Older chatbots followed a fixed script: pick "opening hours" or "bookings" from a menu. An AI chatbot uses a [large language model (LLM)](/insights/glossary/large-language-model-llm) to read what a person wrote and compose a reply, so people can ask in their own words. What it knows and what it is allowed to do is decided by the business that sets it up. Setting one up is mostly choices rather than code: the one job it does, the approved content it answers from, and the point where it hands over to a person.

Common forms:

- A website chatbot that answers customer questions or takes enquiries.
- An internal chatbot where staff ask about policies, procedures or product details.
- A chatbot inside a messaging app that customers already use.

The words overlap, so here is how we use them. "Chatbot" describes the format: a conversation. An [AI assistant](/insights/glossary/ai-assistant) is a tool you talk to for help with everyday work, and an [AI agent](/insights/glossary/ai-agent) works through several steps towards a goal by itself. A chatbot can be a simple question answerer or an assistant, and some agents sit behind a chat window. The useful question is what the tool may do beyond replying.

A chatbot can give a wrong answer with full confidence, which is called [hallucination](/insights/glossary/hallucination). The risk goes down when the chatbot answers only from the business's approved content (see [retrieval-augmented generation](/insights/glossary/retrieval-augmented-generation-rag)), has one narrow job, says it is an AI, hands over to a person when it is unsure, and has its conversations reviewed. It does not go to zero, so keep a [human in the loop](/insights/glossary/human-in-the-loop).

If a chatbot collects customer details, the data rules matter. The PDPC has published [advisory guidelines on the use of personal data in AI recommendation and decision systems](https://pdpc.gov.sg/guidelines-and-consultation/2024/02/advisory-guidelines-on-use-of-personal-data-in-ai-recommendation-and-decision-systems), which cover using personal data to train or develop AI and what to tell people when asking for consent (page read 16 Nov 2026). The page does not mention chatbots by name, so check how it applies to yours. Our term on [personal data under the PDPA](/insights/glossary/personal-data-under-the-pdpa) is a plain starting point.

## An example

A 12-person physiotherapy clinic puts a chatbot on its website. It answers questions about opening hours, treatments and how to book, using only the clinic's own approved FAQ text. When someone asks about their own condition, or something the FAQ does not cover, it says so and passes the message to the front desk. Once a week the receptionist reads the questions it could not answer and adds them to the FAQ.
