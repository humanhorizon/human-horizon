## What it looks like at work

A useful pilot is narrow and honest. It usually follows these steps:

1. **Pick one process** that recurs often and slows people down.
2. **Define success upfront**, such as time per task, turnaround or how often work needs correcting.
3. **Measure the starting point** before anything changes.
4. **Run it with a small group** on their real work, with a person checking every output.
5. **Compare and decide**: scale it, change it or stop it.

Gradual rollout is also what Singapore's guidance recommends for AI agents. IMDA's [factsheet on the Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI](https://www.imda.gov.sg/-/media/imda/files/news-and-events/media-room/media-releases/2026/01/factsheet-model-ai-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai.pdf) recommends rolling agents out by limiting them to certain users or features first, with continuous monitoring and testing.

In our view, the common failure is the pilot that never ends. It runs, people like it, and then nothing changes. This tends to happen when nobody owns the decision, success was never defined, or the pilot used made-up tasks instead of real ones.

## An example

A logistics company pilots AI-drafted shipment status replies with three customer service staff on one large account. Before starting, they record how long each reply takes and how often replies need fixing. During the pilot, each staff member checks every draft against the tracking system before sending. At the end, the team compares the numbers with the starting point and decides whether to extend the workflow to other accounts.

## AI pilot vs proof of concept

A proof of concept tests whether something can work at all, often on sample data. A pilot tests whether it works in daily work, with real people and real pressure. Both are useful. Only a pilot tells you whether people will keep using it.
